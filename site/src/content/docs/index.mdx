You **dig**. You don't read 2,000 commits — you read a book the agent writes for you. Then you sit with it and ask the questions that turn information into understanding.

## Read it like a book — chapter by chapter

A book is structured. It has chapters. Each chapter has a thesis, evidence, and the
parts the author chose to leave out. You read books *critically* — you mark margins,
you push back on the framing, you check the citations.

That's exactly the loop. Run `/dig` in Claude Code, OpenCode, or Cursor, in any repo
with git history.

<ol class="field-steps">
  <li>
    <strong>Survey the dig site</strong>
    Commits, contributors, releases, RFCs, infra files, the suspicious silence around <code>legacy_v2/</code>. Where's the rich seam on this project?
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Identify the chapters</strong>
    Team transitions, migrations, the Great Refactor of 2022, the incident that shall not be named. Each one a chapter you'll read.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Deep-dive, one chapter at a time</strong>
    What happened, who did it, why, and what got abandoned along the way. Enough context to ask good questions — not a wall of text.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Push back. Hard.</strong>
    <em>Why not the simpler thing? What breaks if this is wrong? Was this eventually replaced? Whose call was this and what were they optimising for?</em> The chapter writes itself into <code>chapters/&lt;n&gt;.md</code> as you talk.
  </li>
</ol>

<p class="field-pullquote">
  <strong>Step 4 is the whole point.</strong> Steps 1–3 produce a draft — a thing to
  react to. Real understanding lives in the conversation: the assumptions you challenge,
  the alternatives you forced into the open, the answers that don't appear in any commit
  message. <em>Reading is the prep. Arguing is the learning.</em>
</p>

### Questions worth asking out loud

The agent will happily answer these. Skip them at your peril.

<ul class="question-list">
  <li><em>Why not the simpler thing?</em> — Forces the agent to defend the existing complexity. Half the time it can't, and you've found dead weight.</li>
  <li><em>What breaks if this assumption is wrong?</em> — Builds operational intuition you cannot get from reading code.</li>
  <li><em>Was this eventually replaced?</em> — If yes, the replacement is the verdict on the original trade-off.</li>
  <li><em>Who pushed for this and what were they optimising for?</em> — Decisions are people. Names + motivations explain shape that code can't.</li>
  <li><em>What got abandoned in this chapter?</em> — Casualties often explain the surviving design better than the surviving design does.</li>
</ul>

